The ABEM Terrameter LS 2 deploys across Dunedin terrain with four steel electrodes hammered into volcanic loess or schist-derived soils. We run a Schlumberger array expanding from 1.5 m out to 200 m AB/2, injecting controlled DC current while the potential dipole measures voltage decay at each spacing. The cable reels unspool along Baldwin Street gradients or across Taieri Plain paddocks, and the resistivity meter logs apparent resistivity at 18 to 24 depth levels per sounding. With a 250 W transmitter and 2.5 A maximum output, the unit punches through the high-contact-resistance surfaces common on Otago Peninsula basalt outcrops. Each VES takes roughly 45 minutes once the spread is laid, yielding a one-dimensional resistivity profile that our team processes using IX1D inversion software before the crew even packs up the field truck.

A single VES sounding can map the basalt bedrock interface through 40 metres of saturated Dunedin colluvium in under an hour.

Methodology applied in Dunedin

NZS 3404 and the NZGS guideline for geophysical site investigation frame every VES campaign we execute in Dunedin. The city sits on a complex patchwork of Dunedin Volcanic Group rocks, Leith Valley alluvium, and harbour-side reclaimed land, so resistivity contrasts between basalt flows, saturated gravels, and marine clays are often sharp and diagnostic. We calibrate each sounding against borehole logs or test pit exposures where accessible, converting ohm-metre values to lithological boundaries with better than 90% correlation when a local ground-truth tie exists. Moisture in the loess mantle across South Dunedin and Caversham creates a low-resistivity skin that masks deeper structure—our processing strips that near-surface effect using constrained smooth-model inversion so contractors get a clean basement profile for foundation depth decisions. The method works particularly well for mapping the depth to unweathered basalt, which typically reads above 200 Ωm against 20–50 Ωm in the overlying saturated colluvium.

Local geotechnical conditions in Dunedin

Saturated harbour fill along the Dunedin waterfront reads below 10 Ωm and can screen deeper resistivity contrasts from foundation rock. Marine clay lenses trapped within the reclaimed zone produce the same low-resistivity signature as saline groundwater, so interpreting a VES without a borehole tie risks misclassifying soft compressible clay as competent gravel. Winter campaigns on the Taieri Plain must contend with surface ponding after sustained rain; waterlogged ground reduces electrode coupling resistance but also flattens the resistivity contrast between sand and silt layers, suppressing the very signal we need to pick stratigraphic boundaries. Basalt flow contacts on Otago Peninsula weather to conductive clay rinds that exaggerate apparent bedrock depth by 2–4 metres if the inversion model is not constrained with a priori layer limits.

Our services

Each VES project in Dunedin runs as a standalone campaign or integrated into a broader geotechnical investigation. The electrical approach complements mechanical drilling by filling spatial gaps between boreholes with continuous resistivity cross-sections.

Groundwater exploration VES

We map aquifer depth and salinity boundaries across the Taieri Basin and North Dunedin alluvial corridors, identifying fresh-water lenses above brackish intrusion for rural supply and irrigation bore siting.

Bedrock profiling for foundation design

Resistivity soundings locate the top of competent basalt or schist beneath colluvial cover on hillside sections, giving structural engineers a reliable basement contour before footing excavation begins.

Reclaimed land stratigraphy

We run tight VES grids over harbour-side reclamation to differentiate fill, marine sediment, and natural substrate, feeding settlement and liquefaction assessments under NZS 4203 loading combinations.

Cross-hole resistivity tomography support

Surface VES soundings provide starting resistivity models for 2D ERT lines, reducing inversion ambiguity when imaging paleochannels or buried volcanic features beneath Dunedin's CBD.

Common questions

How much does a VES survey cost in Dunedin?

A single vertical electrical sounding in Dunedin generally runs between NZ$1,010 and NZ$1,740 per station, depending on the maximum AB/2 spread, terrain access, and whether a borehole tie is available for calibration. A grid of four to six soundings across a typical residential section brings the total campaign cost into the mid-four-figure range, which is often less than a single mud-rotary borehole when the objective is mapping bedrock depth between scattered drill points.

How deep can VES penetrate in Dunedin's volcanic geology?

With a Schlumberger array expanding to AB/2 = 200 m, we typically resolve interfaces down to 40–60 metres in Dunedin volcanic and alluvial settings. The practical depth limit is controlled by the resistivity contrast between layers rather than the cable spread alone. High-resistivity basalt flows can be traced deeper, while conductive saturated colluvium attenuates current penetration and caps effective investigation at the shallow end of that range.

Is VES reliable for locating groundwater in the Taieri Basin?

Yes, it is one of the most cost-effective reconnaissance methods for the Taieri Basin's layered alluvial aquifer system. We calibrate resistivity soundings against existing bore logs from the Otago Regional Council database, tying low-resistivity zones to specific water-bearing gravels. The method also picks the freshwater-saline interface in coastal parts of the basin, which is critical for avoiding brackish production when siting irrigation wells. More info.
