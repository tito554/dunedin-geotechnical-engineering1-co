The subsurface profile beneath the Dunedin CBD, with its deep harbour silts and reclaimed land, bears little resemblance to the schist bedrock that anchors the hill suburbs of Maori Hill or Roslyn. Where one site demands careful mapping of soft, compressible layers that amplify ground motion, another requires precise definition of the rockhead profile for efficient foundation design. Seismic tomography bridges this gap, offering a continuous velocity model of the ground rather than isolated point data. For projects navigating the transition from the Leith Valley alluvium to the volcanic breccias of the Dunedin Volcanic Complex, the method reveals lateral and vertical changes that boreholes alone can miss. The resulting images guide everything from excavation planning to seismic site classification under NZS 1170.5, and they integrate naturally with MASW surveys when a complete near-surface velocity profile is required for site-specific response analysis.

A tomography survey does not just find the bedrock; it quantifies the velocity contrast that controls how seismic waves refract and amplify beneath a structure.

Methodology applied in Dunedin

Dunedin’s growth from a small whaling settlement into a university city has forced development onto increasingly challenging terrain, from the steep slopes of the Town Belt to the engineered fills of the Southern Motorway corridor. This expansion brought geotechnical surprises: variable volcanic lithologies interbedded with alluvial and marine sediments that defy simple layer-cake models. Seismic tomography responds to this complexity by measuring travel times along dense geophone arrays, then inverting the data to produce a two- or three-dimensional velocity model. The team uses a 24- to 48-channel seismograph with both sledgehammer and weight-drop sources, adapting the acquisition geometry to the target depth. For road widening along the Otago Peninsula, for example, we deploy long refraction spreads to identify cavities in the basalt flows, while reflection surveys on the Taieri Plain map the Quaternary sediment-basement interface with metre-scale precision. Each profile is processed with iterative ray-tracing algorithms that honour the irregular topography so common across the Dunedin basin.

Local geotechnical conditions in Dunedin

The Dunedin region sits within a zone of moderate seismicity, with the Akatore and Titri faults capable of producing significant ground shaking. The city’s underlying geology compounds the risk: unconsolidated Quaternary alluvium and soft estuarine deposits trap and amplify seismic energy, as documented in the Otago Regional Council hazard assessments. A velocity model derived from seismic tomography provides the direct input for site-class determination per NZS 1170.5, moving beyond the default assumptions of code-based soil factors. When a profile reveals a sharp velocity inversion — a faster layer overlying slower material — the potential for wave trapping exists, a condition that linear-equivalent analysis often misses. Equally critical for hillside developments, the method delineates the thickness of colluvium mantling the slopes; where this mantle is thin and saturated, rainfall-triggered debris flows become a credible scenario. Knowing the geometry and stiffness of these layers before excavation begins is the difference between a controlled cut and a costly remedial stabilisation.

Our services

Every seismic tomography campaign in Dunedin is designed around the specific geological question — whether that is mapping the rockhead beneath a proposed building platform or imaging fault strands across a linear infrastructure corridor. The two core techniques we employ are described below.

Seismic Refraction Tomography

Refraction surveys are suited to sites where velocity increases with depth, a common scenario in the Dunedin area where weathered volcanic rock grades into competent basalt or schist. We lay out 48- to 96-metre spreads with single-geophone-per-channel recording, acquiring multiple shot points to ensure dense ray coverage. The tomographic inversion produces a continuous Vp cross-section that identifies rippability boundaries, fracture density trends, and buried paleo-channels. This information is routinely used to plan bulk-earthwork programs and to position deep foundations where rock quality is highest.

Seismic Reflection Tomography

Where the target is a deep interface or a velocity inversion that refraction cannot resolve, reflection profiling becomes the tool of choice. Using common-midpoint (CMP) acquisition with higher-fold coverage, we image the stratigraphy beneath the Dunedin harbour margin and the Taieri Basin sediments. Post-stack depth migration corrects for the steep dips encountered near the Titri Fault scarp. The final sections are calibrated against available borehole logs and delivered as depth-converted profiles that structural geologists and geotechnical engineers can interpret together.

Common questions

What is the difference between seismic refraction and reflection for a Dunedin site?

Refraction measures the time for seismic waves to travel along the top of a faster layer; it requires velocity to increase with depth, which suits many Dunedin sites where weathered volcanics overlie fresher rock. Reflection records echoes from interfaces regardless of the velocity trend, so it can image a soft layer beneath a hard one. In practice, we often combine both: refraction for the upper 30 m and reflection for deeper targets such as the sediment-basement contact beneath the Taieri Plain.

How long does a typical seismic tomography survey take in Dunedin?

A single refraction line of 100 metres with multiple shot points can be acquired in half a day, including setup and breakdown. A reflection profile of similar length with higher fold coverage may require a full day. Processing and interpretation add three to five business days, though preliminary velocity sections can be shared within 48 hours for urgent earthworks decisions.

Can seismic tomography work on paved surfaces or inside buildings?

Yes, with some limitations. On asphalt or concrete, we use coupling plates and a weight-drop source to avoid surface damage. Inside existing structures, space constraints may limit spread length, but shorter, high-resolution lines are feasible. For industrial sites with high background noise, a more powerful source and longer recording times are used to maintain signal quality.

What does a seismic tomography survey cost in the Dunedin area?

For a typical Dunedin site, seismic tomography surveys range from NZ$3,990 for a short refraction line with rapid processing to NZ$9,520 for a combined refraction-reflection programme covering several hundred metres with full tomographic inversion and a comprehensive report. The final figure depends on line length, number of shots, terrain difficulty, and the depth of investigation required.

Is seismic tomography sufficient for a building consent application in Dunedin?

Geophysical data alone rarely satisfies all consent requirements; it is most powerful when integrated with borehole logs, test pits, and laboratory testing. The Dunedin City Council accepts seismic tomography as part of a site investigation package when it is accompanied by a factual report signed by a CPEng geotechnical engineer and cross-calibrated with direct investigation data. We routinely collaborate with local engineering consultancies to deliver a complete, consent-ready submission.
